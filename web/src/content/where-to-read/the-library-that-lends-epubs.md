Most people who ask where to get books forget the largest legal library in the world: the
public one. In many countries a library card includes free ebooks, lent as EPUBs with a
return date, through apps like Libby or OverDrive.

## How the loan works

You join a library (often online, with an address in its area), add the card to the Libby or
OverDrive app, and borrow a title. The book appears as a loan with an expiry — usually 7, 14
or 21 days. At the end, it returns itself and disappears from the shelf.

There is no purchase, no subscription to the lending app, and no account on a book site. It is
a library, on a phone.

## What DRM means here

Library EPUBs are usually protected by Adobe DRM or the provider's own scheme. That means the
file opens in the app that borrowed it, and cannot simply be copied into another reader. This
is not a technical failure to work around; it is the lending agreement.

The practical rule: read the loan in the app that owns it, and use a reader app for the
files you own outright — public-domain EPUBs, purchases without DRM, and your own documents.

## When a loan is the right choice

- A book you will read once and never need to keep.
- A book you are not sure about — borrow first, buy if it earns a second pass.
- Research reading across several books at once, where buying all of them would be absurd.

## When buying is the right choice

- Books you will annotate and return to: a loan's highlights may not survive the return.
- Books you want offline for a trip longer than the loan.
- Anything a library does not carry.

## The gap between the two

A reader that opens 34 formats is useful precisely because the legal sources are scattered:
an EPUB from a friend, a PDF from a university, a public-domain classic, a DRM-free purchase.
The point of the app is not to sell you books; it is to open the ones you have, legibly, on
the screen you actually carry.
