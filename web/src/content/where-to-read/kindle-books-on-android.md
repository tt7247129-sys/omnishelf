The short answer: **for books you bought on Amazon, you need the Kindle app.** That is not
a limitation of any third-party reader. It is what the purchase is: a licence to read the
book inside Amazon's software, with a lock that only that software holds the key to.

The longer answer is worth reading, because three other categories of "Kindle books" are
not locked, and they can be read anywhere.

## Why a Kindle file will not open elsewhere

A book bought from Amazon arrives in a format (AZW, AZW3 or KFX) which is usually wrapped in
Amazon's DRM. The wrapper is not part of the file format; it is encryption around it. A
reader that supports AZW3 — plenty do — can read an unprotected AZW3 file and will simply
fail on a protected one.

Removing that protection is not something this guide will walk you through. It is against
Amazon's terms of service, and in many countries it is a separate legal issue from the
copyright question. If the book is important to you, the honest options are: read it in the
Kindle app, or buy a version you can actually own from a shop that sells EPUBs without a
lock.

## What is not locked

**Public-domain books from Amazon.** Amazon sells free editions of books in the public
domain. Some are delivered without DRM, in which case the file opens anywhere. Others are
locked anyway, because the lock is applied by the purchase path rather than by the book.
Check before assuming.

**Personal documents you send yourself.** Amazon's "Send to Kindle" accepts your own files
and delivers them to your devices. If you download a DRM-free EPUB from a public-domain
catalogue, converting it to a format the Kindle understands is legitimate and easy.

**Books from other shops.** Kobo, Google Play Books and many independent shops sell EPUBs.
Some of those shops add their own lock; many do not. A DRM-free EPUB is the most portable
book file that exists.

## Three legitimate ways to read on Android

### 1. Use the Kindle app for Kindle purchases

It is the only software that can open them, it is free, and its reading features are good.
Keep it for that purpose. The annoyance is not the app; it is that the library is not
yours.

### 2. Move your own files to a reader that respects them

For DRM-free EPUB, PDF, MOBI, AZW3, FB2, DJVU, CBZ and DOCX, any capable Android reader
works. A reader worth using keeps a real folder structure, remembers your place, and lets
you highlight without asking for an account. That is the case the rest of this site is
about.

### 3. Keep two apps, deliberately

One for what Amazon sells you, one for everything else. It sounds untidy; it is what most
heavy readers actually do, and it avoids the trap of trying to make one app do something
its licence does not allow.

## A note on "converting Kindle books"

Search results are full of tools promising to convert Kindle files. Two things are true at
once: conversion is trivial for DRM-free files, and for DRM-protected files the tool has to
defeat the encryption, which is the part that carries the legal risk. If you are converting
a file you made yourself, you are fine. If you are converting a purchase, read the terms
before you install anything.

## What this means for choosing a reader

If your library is Kindle purchases, your reader is the Kindle app — no third-party app
will change that. If your library is a folder of files, choose the app that opens the most
formats and asks the least of you. Those are different problems, and the mistake is buying
a "Kindle alternative" expecting it to open a locked library.
