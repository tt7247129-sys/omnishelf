SQ3R is a study technique from the 1940s: survey, question, read, recite, review. Written
as five steps on a worksheet, it is tedious. Written as habits, it is how careful reading
already works — with one step most readers skip.

## Survey

Five minutes with the headings, figures and first sentences. The point is not to understand
the chapter; it is to know its shape, so that when you read you can put each paragraph in a
place.

## Question

Turn each heading into a question you want answered. "Causes of the collapse" becomes "what
did the author think caused it, and does the evidence hold?" This step is cheap and it
changes what you notice, because a question is a filter.

## Read

One section at a time, not one chapter at a time. Stop at the end of each section and look
away from the page for a second. If you cannot say what the section argued, you did not read
it; you scanned it.

## Recite

Say it out loud, in your own words, without looking. This is uncomfortable, which is why it
is skipped. It is also the step that produces memory: retrieving a thing is what stores it,
and reading it again is not.

## Review

At the end of the chapter, go back through the questions you wrote. Answer them. Then, a day
later, answer them again without the book. That second attempt is the whole method.

## The one that actually matters

If you only keep one habit from this list, keep recite. A reader can survey, question and
review in their head, but there is no substitute for saying the idea out loud before checking
the page.

In the reader, the review step has a home: highlights and notes stay attached to the book, so
the material is still there when you come back to it — which is the only version of review
that survives a busy week.
