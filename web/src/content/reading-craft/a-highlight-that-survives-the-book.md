A highlight is not a bookmark. A bookmark says *here*; a highlight should say *why here*. If
you cannot tell, a month later, what a mark was for, it has already failed.

## The quote

The sentence worth keeping for its wording, not just its idea. Keep it short — a quote you
cannot retype from memory is a quote you will never use. This is the mark to make for the
share card, and the one most readers overuse.

## The question

A place where you do not understand something, or do not believe it. Marking a question is
more valuable than marking agreement, because questions are what a second pass is for. A
book with no questions in the margin was probably not read closely.

## The objection

The place where you disagree. Keep these separate from quotes, ideally by colour. An
objection recorded at the time is the beginning of an argument you can have later; an
objection remembered is a mood.

## Why four colours, not fourteen

A colour scheme with fourteen options is a filing system you will stop using in a week. The
reader ships four: one for quotes, one for questions, one for objections, and one for things
that belong to another book. That is enough to make the return pass fast, and few enough to
remember.

## The full-page problem

A page highlighted end to end has no information in it: it says *this whole page mattered*,
which is the same as saying nothing mattered. The limit is not a rule, but a test — if the
highlight covers more than a line or two, write a note instead and highlight the sentence
that carries the point.

## Where the marks live

A mark is useful in proportion to how easily it can be found again. Marks that leave the
book — copied into a separate app, exported to a file, photographed — become a second
library you will not maintain. Keeping highlights attached to the book is the difference
between a marked-up shelf and a searchable one.
