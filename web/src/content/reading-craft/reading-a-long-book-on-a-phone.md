A phone is a poor page: narrow, bright, and always one notification away. It is also the page
most people have. These are the six settings that decide which of those two facts wins.

## Line length

The comfortable range is 35–60 characters. Wider and the eye loses the start of the line;
narrower and every second word is hyphenated. On a phone this usually means a smaller margin
and a slightly smaller font than feels right at first — the page should feel a little too
wide for the screen.

## Font, and the size that is actually right

Start at a size where a paragraph is four to six lines. Then read a page and see whether you
notice the font at all; if you do, it is wrong in one direction or the other. A serif is
easier for long prose; a sans is easier for reference.

## Margins

Twenty-pixel margins on a 390-pixel screen waste a tenth of the page. Reduce them until the
text almost reaches the edge, then put them back by a few pixels so the eye has somewhere to
land.

## Page mode or scroll

Paged reading gives you a right-hand edge, which is a progress cue; scrolling gives you a
continuous column, which suits PDFs and anything you will search. A novel on a phone is
easier paged; a manual is easier scrolled.

## Tap zones

A one-handed reader needs the whole right side of the screen to be "next page", and the left
edge to be "back" — not the other way round. Defaults that assume a right-handed grip from
2010 are the most common cause of accidental page turns.

## What happens when you put it down

The page you come back to should be the page you left, including the scroll position in a
long PDF and the point in a chapter where you stopped listening. A reader that loses your
place has failed at the one thing a book does not.

None of these settings is interesting on its own. Together they are the difference between
a phone that can hold a novel and a phone that can only hold notifications.
