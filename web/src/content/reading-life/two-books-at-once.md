Two books at once is normal; two books at once in the same hour is not. The useful version of
parallel reading pairs books that ask different things of you, so that one is never competing
with the other for the same attention.

## The pairing rule

Pair a book that carries you (novel, memoir, narrative history) with a book that asks you to
work (philosophy, technical, essays). Read the carrying book when you are tired, the working
book when you are not. The pairing does the scheduling for you.

## Different hours, different postures

A novel belongs to the last thirty minutes of the day, in the chair, on the page. A working
book belongs to a desk and a morning. When the hours are distinct, the two books stop being
competition and start being a rhythm.

## Why three is one too many

With three, one book is always in the way of the other two, and it is usually the hardest one.
Two is the number where each book has a clear job. Three is a wish list with bookmarks.

## Keeping them apart

The physical version of this advice is two bookmarks and a rule about where each book lives.
The digital version is simpler and stricter: two books in two tabs, never one tab with three.
Closing a tab is a decision; leaving it open is a postponement.

## When one book starts losing

If you find yourself reading book A to avoid book B, B is not working. Give B two pages to
convince you; if it does not, close it. That is not abandoning a book — it is choosing the one
you are actually reading.

## The point

Parallel reading is not a way to read more; it is a way to match the book to the hour. One
carrying book and one working book, in different hours, is the whole system.
