The mistake is to shop for brightness. A bedside reading light that is too dim is annoying;
a light that is bright, wide and cool-white is a different kind of problem, because it
wakes the other person and it tells your body it is noon.

We spent a fortnight reading in bed with three different lights and a cheap lux meter,
which is not a scientific study but is enough to see where the design trade-offs actually
sit.

## Two users, one lamp

The page wants enough light to make 11-point text comfortable: on a matte paperback, a few
hundred lux at the page is plenty. The room wants as little as possible.

Those are not in conflict if the light is directional. A narrow beam aimed at the page puts
the light where it is needed and leaves the rest of the room dark. A wide beam at the same
brightness lights the page *and* the pillow next to you, which is why "bright but not
waking anyone" is impossible with a bare bulb and easy with a hooded clip lamp.

The measurement that surprised us: at the page, the clip lamp with a hooded reflector gave
about the same reading as a wide desk lamp four times its rated output, because the desk
lamp was spilling most of its light onto the wall. Lux at the page is the number that
matters; the number on the box is not.

## Colour temperature is the other half

Warm white — somewhere around 2700 to 3000K — is the usual recommendation for evening
reading, and the reason is not aesthetic. Short-wavelength light in the evening suppresses
melatonin and shifts circadian timing; the general guidance from sleep research is to
reduce blue-rich light in the hours before bed. A 6500K lamp at reading distance is the
worst version of this, and it is what most cheap clip lamps ship as the default.

The practical rule we ended up with: one warm setting, and no need for a colour picker. If
a lamp offers five temperatures, the other four exist for photographs.

There is a second-order effect that matters more for reading than for sleep. Cool light
raises contrast on white paper, which feels sharper; warm light lowers it, which feels
softer and, at low levels, makes small text harder to resolve. The comfortable compromise
is warm light at a higher level rather than cool light at a low one.

## What we changed, and what we would not buy again

Three things survived the fortnight.

**A clip with a real hood.** The physical shade does more for the sleeping partner than any
dimming curve. If the lamp's beam is a soft sphere, no setting will keep it off the pillow.

**A step dimmer with a very low bottom step.** Most lamps' lowest setting is still bright
enough to light the room. The useful range for bedside reading is the bottom two steps, and
a lamp whose bottom step is 30% is a lamp with one usable setting.

**A warm fixed temperature.** One good 3000K setting beats five mediocre ones, because the
decision is made once rather than every night.

What we would not buy again: a lamp that clips to the book itself. It works for a
paperback and fails for anything thick enough to need a stand, and the weight on the cover
is exactly where a paperback's binding is weakest.

## The phone as a reading light

The obvious alternative is the phone, and it fails for a reason that has nothing to do with
light quality: a phone screen illuminates the reader's face, not the page. At the distance
you hold a book, the spill from a phone is a fraction of what the page needs, and you end
up turning the brightness up until the screen is the brightest object in the room — which
is the opposite of the goal.

For reading a phone, the screen *is* the page and the problem disappears. For reading
paper, it does not.

## What the other person actually receives

lamp on its lowest useful setting, aimed at a book held about forty centimetres from the
face:

With the hooded clip lamp, the reading at the pillow was low enough that the meter's bottom
range was the interesting part — the beam was falling almost entirely on the page and the
reader's hands.

With a bare desk lamp at the same page brightness, the pillow reading was several times
higher, because the light was going everywhere including the wall behind the book.

With a phone at comfortable screen brightness, the pillow reading was higher than either,
because the screen faces the room.

That last result is the counter-intuitive one. The device people reach for as a reading
light is the worst option for the person next to them, and the reason is geometry: a screen
emits toward the room, a hooded lamp emits toward a page.

## The lamp and the e-reader are not the same problem

If you read on an e-ink device with a front light, everything above still applies to the
room but not to the page: the device lights itself, and the lamp becomes unnecessary.

That is a genuine advantage and it has its own cost. The front light on most readers uses a
light guide that sits above the panel, and at high settings it reduces effective contrast —
the text gets greyer as the light gets brighter. The usable range is the lower third of the
slider, which is also the range where the colour temperature setting actually matters.

The practical conclusion is that an e-reader replaces the lamp for the page but not for the
room: at a low front-light setting you can read without waking anyone, and you can do it
without a lamp at all. A phone cannot make that claim, because its light faces outward.

## Two small things that mattered more than the lamp

After the fortnight, the change we kept was not the lamp. It was moving the book.

Reading propped up at the usual angle meant the lamp had to be aimed across the bed, which
put it behind the reader's shoulder and made the page shadowy. Reading with the book
lower, closer to the lamp, meant less spill and a more even page — and the shadow problem
disappeared because the light was no longer coming from behind.

The second was a blackout curtain. Not for the reading, for the room: a room that is dark
apart from one lamp makes the lamp's spill obvious; a room with street light coming through
the curtain makes it invisible, and the sleeping person notices the contrast rather than
the light.

Neither of these is a product, and together they did more than the difference between the
lamps we tested. That is a common shape in this column: the accessory is real, but it is
rarely the first variable, and buying the second-best version of the right shape is better
than buying the best version of the wrong one.

## Where this advice stops

This is not medical advice, and it is not about eye strain, which has more to do with
accommodation and blink rate than with colour temperature. If reading at night is causing
headaches or disrupting sleep in a way that persists, the lamp is not the variable to
change.

It also does not apply to e-ink readers with front lights, which have their own trade-offs:
the light guide makes the screen glow evenly, but at high settings it reduces contrast, and
the warm settings on some devices are warmer than any bedside lamp.

The thing we took from this into the reader: when someone reads at night on a phone, the
app's own brightness control matters more than the system's, because the system slider's
bottom step is still too bright in a dark room. Our app brightness goes lower than the
system's, and it is the setting people mention most often when they describe reading in bed.
