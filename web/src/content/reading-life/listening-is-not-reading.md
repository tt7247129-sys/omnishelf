Listening and reading are different instruments. Treating them as interchangeable is how
people end up disappointed by one and suspicious of the other.

## What listening keeps

Narrative, voice, momentum. A novel at 1.3× while walking is the same book experienced at a
different grain. For books that are already stories — plot, character, argument carried by
rhythm — listening works.

## What listening loses

Structure you can see at a glance: headings, lists, tables, the shape of a chapter on a page.
It also loses the pause. A reader stops at the end of a paragraph because the page ends;
audio keeps going, and an idea that deserved two minutes gets eight seconds.

## Names and numbers

Spoken text flattens what typography distinguishes. Footnotes disappear, tables become
sentences, and a sentence with three clauses becomes one long clause. For technical books
this is not a small loss — it is the book.

## The good pairing

Use audio for the second pass through a book you have already read on the page: the
structure is in your head, so the voice does not need to carry it. Use the page for the first
pass, where you need to see the shape.

Use audio for the commute, the walk and the washing-up — time that was not going to be
reading time. Keep the page for the chair that was.

## One book, two forms

The most useful version is not "audio or text" but both on the same file: the same book, at
the same place, in whichever mode the moment allows. That is a technical question about
keeping the position in sync — and a good reason to have the reader own both modes on one
file rather than outsourcing listening to a separate service.

## The honest summary

Listening is reading's sibling, not its replacement. It gets you through more books; the page
gets you further into them. Keeping both available on the same phone is the point.
